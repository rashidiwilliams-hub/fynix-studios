How to Actually Taste Rum

A short guide to tasting rum properly — no swirling required.

A short guide to tasting rum properly — no swirling required. Start by nosing the glass from a distance before bringing it closer; rum's alcohol content can overwhelm your nose if you dive in too fast. Take a small sip and let it coat your tongue before swallowing. Add a few drops of water if the rum feels closed off — it can open up notes of molasses and spice that neat pours hide. Most importantly, taste slowly. There's no prize for finishing first.

Why Cask Type Matters

The barrel does more work than most drinkers realize.

The barrel does more work than most drinkers realize. Ex-bourbon casks tend to bring vanilla and caramel notes, while ex-sherry casks add dried fruit and spice. The age of the cask matters too — a barrel that's already been used several times imparts less flavor than a fresh one. That's part of why two whiskies made from the same spirit can taste completely different after maturation. The cask isn't just storage; it's an ingredient.

Rum Cocktails Beyond the Old Fashioned

A few lesser-known ways to mix rum worth trying.

A few lesser-known ways to mix rum worth trying. A Dark 'n' Stormy pairs spiced or dark rum with ginger beer and a squeeze of lime for something sharp and refreshing. A Painkiller blends dark rum with pineapple, orange juice, and cream of coconut for a rich, tropical pour. And a classic Rum Old Fashioned — sugar, bitters, and a generous pour — is criminally underordered. Give one of these a try next time you're mixing at home.
